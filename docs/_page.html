Exact-run prediction is empirically very difficult in this dataset. The model matches the exact next-over total about 8.3% of the time. Among similar observed match states the outcome standard deviation is still 4.60 against an overall 5.14 — which suggests a large fraction of the variation is either intrinsically stochastic or driven by context absent from the current feature set.

Event probabilities

Modest ranking, well-calibrated numbers.

These are two different questions, and this system answers the second one much better than the first.

Discrimination — measured by AUC — asks how well the model ranks high-risk situations above low-risk ones. Calibration — measured by expected calibration error — asks whether a stated probability matches the observed frequency.

If the model repeatedly assigns roughly a 30% wicket probability to similar overs, a well-calibrated model should see a wicket in roughly 30% of those cases. That is a statement about the group, not a promise about any single over.

What this means

AUC between 0.60 and 0.65 is weak ranking — only modestly better than chance. But ECE at or below 0.0135 means the probabilities themselves are trustworthy as probabilities. The model is poor at telling you which over will produce a wicket, and good at telling you how often overs like this one do.

Uncertainty

Intervals that over-cover, reported as such.

The champion produces an 80% prediction interval by split conformal calibration on a held-out block. On the 2026 season its observed coverage is 86.6% against a nominal 80% target.

That is over-coverage: the interval is wider than it needs to be, so it captures the truth more often than advertised. Part of this comes from rounding the displayed bounds outward to whole runs. It is a real miscalibration and is not presented as a strength — and coverage is not accuracy.

System architecture

Two paths, one feature contract.

The production path produces expected runs and event probabilities. The auxiliary path produces the full distribution. Both read the same leakage-safe feature builder, so training and serving cannot drift apart.

Experiment · delivery-level probabilistic simulation

An auxiliary distribution model — and a failed primary hypothesis.

The champion optimises a conditional mean. The hypothesis was that modelling each delivery's outcome distribution and simulating the over forward would recover information about explosive overs that a mean-optimiser misses.

Four compatible outcome heads — batter runs, extras, legality, wicket — feed a cricket-aware Monte Carlo simulator: six legal deliveries end the over so wides and no-balls extend it, strike rotates on odd runs, a chase stops at the target, an innings stops at the tenth wicket. 2,500 draws per over, chosen by convergence testing.

Where it was meant to help, it is worse

On actual 15–19 run overs the champion's MAE is 6.050 against the simulator's 6.488 (n=355). On actual 20+ overs, 11.603 against 12.051 (n=125). The simulator predicts lower than the champion precisely where the real totals are highest.

The technical result worth keeping

Sampling the four heads independently produced cricket that does not exist — byes alongside a boundary, wickets on sixes. Measured on training deliveries only, extras on a legal ball occur only when the batter did not score, and dismissal risk collapses from a lift of 2.66 on a dot ball to effectively zero on a four. Known limitation

The extras head over-fires. Simulated extras run at 0.95 runs per over against a historical 0.46, which inflates simulated totals slightly. This is reported rather than corrected in presentation code.

Decision

Auxiliary probabilistic distribution model. Not promoted: the MAE difference of +0.018 runs has a 95% interval of [−0.0145, +0.0514] and is not significant, explosive-over accuracy regresses, and R² falls. Not rejected: the intervals and every proper scoring rule are genuinely better. The hybrid weight fitted on the calibration season came out at 1.0, collapsing the hybrid onto the simulator, so blending offered nothing distinct.

Methodology note

Why “never predicts 20+” was misleading.

The run-bucket classifier rarely selects 20+ as its highest-probability class. That is not the same as assigning it no probability. Consider a forecast like this one:

0–410%
5–711%
8–1028%
11–1425%
15–1917%
20+9%

The argmax is 8–10, yet the model is assigning a meaningful 9% to an explosive over. Judging it by argmax recall alone records a zero and hides that signal entirely.

Tail behaviour therefore belongs to AUC, PR-AUC, Brier score and calibration — measures that read the whole distribution — rather than argmax recall. Correcting this changed the conclusion of the experiment: the champion already carried tail signal that argmax concealed.

Negative results

What didn’t work.

Five approaches were built, measured and rejected. They are kept here because a results page without them is not a result.

GRU sequence model

Rejected

A recurrent encoder over the last 24 deliveries scored 3.833 validation MAE against the tabular ensemble's 3.798. The best blend moved MAE by 0.0031 runs — 0.7 standard errors of the paired difference, and the blend weight was chosen on that same season. Delivery ordering carried no usable signal here.

Delivery-lag features

Rejected

Thirty-six features encoding the previous twelve deliveries individually. Removing them improved walk-forward MAE by 0.022 runs at 4.8 standard errors — an independent corroboration of the GRU result from a completely different model family.

Naive recency training

Rejected

Training straight through recent seasons with in-season validation scored worse, at −2.4 standard errors. The cause was an 819-over validation block making early stopping unreliable — the confound, not recency itself. A select-then-refit split fixed it properly.

Expanded feature candidate

Not promoted

A 236-feature model with era-relative rates, batting position and EWMA form reached MAE 3.8772 against the champion's 3.8776 — a difference of 0.0004 runs with a bootstrap interval of [−0.020, +0.019]. Statistically indistinguishable, so the champion stayed.

Simulator as point predictor

Not promoted

Lower MAE by 0.018 runs but not significant, worse on explosive overs, worse RMSE and R², and 2.6× the inference cost. Retained only for the distribution and intervals it genuinely improves.

Provenance caveat

The champion was trained from a working tree with 110 uncommitted files, so its commit hash alone does not describe the code that produced it. Provenance recording was hardened afterwards: runs now store the dirty flag, the uncommitted file count and a digest of the diff. The delivery heads were trained from a clean tree and are reproducible from their commit.

Limitations

What this system cannot do.

Player handedness, bowling styleunavailable — not inferred

Pace, spin, handedness and batting-role metadata are absent from the source archive and are deliberately not guessed from player names, so the matchup features that would depend on them do not exist. Latency was measured with explanations enabled on a shared development machine and is dominated by TreeSHAP.

This project makes no headline accuracy claim, because next-over cricket scoring is substantially stochastic, and the value of this system is a calibrated forecast that quantifies its own uncertainty rather than a point estimate that pretends to certainty.

Next

Where the remaining signal probably is.

Feature engineering on the existing ball-by-ball state has reached diminishing returns — the ablation showed only two robust effects across clean folds, and expanding to 236 features moved held-out MAE by 0.0004 runs. The bottleneck is information, not model capacity.

The highest-value additions would be variables the archive does not currently contain: verified player handedness and bowling style, confirmed playing XI and batting order, toss and pitch context, and ground dimensions. Each would be sourced with recorded provenance, and anything unavailable would stay UNKNOWN rather than being imputed.

On the modelling side, fixing the extras head is the clearest defect to close, and would sharpen the simulated distribution the auxiliary model already improves.
